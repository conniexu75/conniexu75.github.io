## Job Market Paper
<details class="paper-card jmp-card">
<summary>
<b><a href="{{ '/assets/pdf/ConnieXu_JMP.pdf' | relative_url }}">Managing the Rising Cost of Science: Supplier Consolidation in the Academic Life Sciences</a></b> <a class="pdf-link" href="{{ '/assets/pdf/ConnieXu_JMP.pdf' | relative_url }}">[PDF]</a><br>
<span class="coauthors">(with <a href="https://rzhang15.github.io/">Ruby Zhang</a>)</span>
<span class="card-foot"><span class="abs-hint"></span></span>
</summary>
<div class="abstract-body">
<p>Scientists cannot easily substitute away from the inputs their experiments depend on or pass on
higher costs, leaving them vulnerable to rising prices in increasingly concentrated markets for life
sciences inputs. How much science is lost when these inputs become more expensive, and which scientists
are better able to adjust? We use the 2014 merger of two large life sciences suppliers as a shock to the
prices principal investigators (PIs) pay for these inputs, drawing on novel purchase-level procurement
records from U.S. public universities linked to PIs’ publications. Prices in affected markets rose by
almost 20 percent, while quantities declined only slightly and temporarily, consistent with highly
inelastic demand. PIs facing larger increases in their input costs published less. For the average PI,
input costs rose by about 2.8 percent and research output fell by about 1.2 percent, implying an
elasticity of −0.42. Higher input prices therefore reduce scientific production, not just raise research
costs. The output losses fell disproportionately on early-career PIs. Financial resources do not fully
explain this difference. Instead, our findings suggest that experience helps PIs adjust to higher prices
through tacit knowledge accumulated during training and years of running a lab.</p>
</div>
</details>
## Working Papers
<details class="paper-card">
<summary>
<b><a href="{{ '/assets/pdf/w33996.pdf' | relative_url }}">Person and Place Effects in Scientific Discovery</a></b> <a class="pdf-link" href="{{ '/assets/pdf/w33996.pdf' | relative_url }}">[PDF]</a><br>
<span class="coauthors">(with <a href="https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/profile.aspx?facId=868461">Amitabh Chandra</a>)</span><br>
NBER Working Paper 33996
<span class="card-foot"><span class="abs-hint"></span> &nbsp;·&nbsp; Media coverage: <a href="https://www.science.org/content/article/large-study-scientists-who-move-their-labs-reveals-how-location-drives-productivity">Science</a></span>
</summary>
<div class="abstract-body">
<p>How much of a scientist's research output is attributable to individual talent, and how much to the institution where they work? Because markets systematically under-provide fundamental science, the answer matters for how societies allocate the resources that substitute for missing market incentives, how the scientific community evaluates researchers for promotion and grants, and whether the current distribution of scientists across institutions maximizes knowledge production. We study this question using citation-weighted publications in leading life-science journals and a movers design that exploits variation in the output of nearly 40,000 scientists who change institutions. We find that 50 to 60 percent of the variation in a scientist's research output is attributable to institutional factors. After correcting for limited mobility bias, we document positive assortative matching between scientists and institutions (a correlation of 0.3 between person and place effects), a necessary condition for maximizing aggregate knowledge production under the multiplicative structure of our model. Around two-thirds of the institutional effect is explained by the presence of star researchers. A scientist's output responds to both the total output of their institution and its funding level, but not to the size of the local research cluster, suggesting that institutional effects operate through the human capital environment within an institution rather than through agglomeration or research funding alone. The symmetry of effects for upward and downward movers implies that institutional advantages do not permanently transfer to scientists who leave. These effects have not diminished despite technologies facilitating remote collaboration, are robust to detailed field-by-year controls, and raise the possibility that similar patterns extend to other areas of fundamental science.</p>
</div>
</details>
<details class="paper-card">
<summary>
<b><a href="https://rzhang15.github.io/assets/pdf/Ruby%20(Ruishan)%20Zhang%20JMP.pdf" style="color: inherit; text-decoration: none;">Returns to Science of Centrally Provisioned GPUs and Optimal Allocation</a></b><br>
<span class="coauthors">(with <a href="https://rzhang15.github.io/">Ruby Zhang</a>)</span>
<span class="card-foot"><span class="abs-hint"></span></span>
</summary>
<div class="abstract-body">
<p>Should federal infrastructure grants go to universities with higher productivity, or to universities
with tighter budgets that cannot self-finance capital investments? We study the allocation of federal
computing grants to U.S. universities by estimating the returns to science of centrally provisioned GPUs
and modeling the crowd-out of private investment. We construct a novel panel of CPU and GPU capacity at
53 U.S. R1 universities with publication counts as our output measure. Exploiting large and lumpy
increases in GPU capacity, we find that AI and deep-learning publications respond more at less-resourced
institutions (restricting to incumbent researchers). To decompose the effect of capital versus labor, we
structurally estimate a production function where multiple fields share a central cluster, and then derive
universities' investment responses to external grants. Holding labor fixed, the observed National Science
Foundation (NSF) portfolio captures 39% of the publication gain attainable with the same budget. The
publication-maximizing allocation shifts dollars from universities with medium-sized endowments toward
both tails. Since the NSF has mandates beyond maximizing publications, we find that the NSF's observed
allocation is more geographically dispersed and consistent with maximizing proportional rather than
absolute gains across universities.</p>
</div>
</details>
<details class="paper-card">
<summary>
<b>The Ripple Effects of Health Care Entry Regulation</b><br>
<span class="coauthors">(with <a href="https://www.yunanji.com/">Yunan Ji</a> and <a href="https://sites.google.com/site/parkerrogersecon">Parker Rogers</a>)</span>
<span class="card-foot"><span class="abs-hint"></span></span>
</summary>
<div class="abstract-body">
<p>We examine how regulatory entry costs for healthcare inputs shape market structure, pricing, and bargaining dynamics throughout the healthcare supply chain. Using a difference-in-differences approach exploiting the FDA's 2016 removal of pre-market clearance requirements for certain medical devices, we find increased manufacturer entry and lower negotiated device prices between manufacturers and hospitals. These reduced input prices propagate downstream, modestly decreasing procedure prices negotiated with insurers and ultimately lowering patient out-of-pocket costs. We then develop and will estimate a structural model that jointly captures manufacturer entry decisions and simultaneous bargaining between hospitals, manufacturers, and insurers. Our proposed counterfactual simulations will quantify how tightening entry regulations or increasing horizontal consolidation would ripple through the supply chain.</p>
</div>
